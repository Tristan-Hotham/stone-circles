Same shape, different millennia

Every stone circle in Europe's open map data, 3,451 records from Portugal to Finland, on one map. Then the harder question: when was each group built? The same simple ring turns up from 6000 BC to AD 400.

Dr Tristan Hotham, Independent researcher · 2026 · Data: OpenStreetMap contributors, Wikidata, p3k14c
3,451stone circles and ship settings in Europe, after cleaning and merging
62%are Iron Age judge's rings in Scandinavia and Finland
20%are the Neolithic and Bronze Age circles of Britain, Ireland, Brittany and Iberia
6,400years between the earliest and latest traditions

Ask anyone to picture a stone circle and they will probably see Stonehenge, or Castlerigg in the rain, or the Ring of Brodgar against an Orkney sky. Stone circles feel British and Irish. Archaeologists count about 1,300 of the Neolithic and Bronze Age kind in Britain, Ireland and Brittany, raised between roughly 3300 BC and 900 BC.

But the ring of upright stones is about the simplest monument there is. You need a field, some boulders and a centre. So I gathered every stone circle I could find in Europe's two big open datasets, OpenStreetMap and Wikidata, cleaned and merged them, and sorted them into the traditions that archaeologists recognise. Then I asked when each tradition was built, using published dates and radiocarbon dates linked to named circles.

The picture that comes back is not an island one. The oldest rings on the map are in the Alentejo in Portugal, begun around 6000 BC. The most numerous, by a wide margin, are the Iron Age domarringar, or judge's rings, of Sweden, built more than two thousand years after Britain's circle tradition began. And the latest are the Gothic circles of Pomerania, still being laid out in the second century AD. Same shape, different millennia.

The ribbon splits into two blocks. The first is Atlantic and early: the Alentejo cromlechs from about 6000 BC, then the circles of Britain, Ireland and Brittany, from about 3300 BC to 900 BC, with most built between 3200 BC and 2000 BC. The second block is late and mostly northern: the little cremation rings of the Pyrenees from about 1200 BC, the ship settings of the Baltic from about 1000 BC, the Swedish and Finnish judge's rings from about 500 BC to AD 400, and the Gothic circles of Pomerania from the later first century AD.

The radiocarbon dates broadly agree with the published ranges, but they are thin outside Britain and Ireland. I linked 356 dates to 43 named circles. Of the 37 circles in Britain and Ireland with linked dates, 30 have a middle date inside 3300 BC to 900 BC. The others are mostly later activity at old sites: burials put into a circle, or a fire lit in it, long after the stones went up. The three dated Pyrenean circles fall between 890 BC and 804 BC, right where the excavators put them. Sweden's thousands of judge's rings have almost no dates in the database at all.

The circle is not one tradition. It is at least two clusters in time, separated by a gap of more than a thousand years in some places and by open sea in others.

03 · WhereMost of the records are Swedish

Counted country by country, Sweden has the most records by far: 2,172, or 48.7 for every 10,000 square kilometres of land, against 569 (23.3) in the United Kingdom and 96 (13.8) in Ireland.

That is partly real and partly the data. 95% of the Swedish records come only from Wikidata, where almost all of them are unlabelled items that look like a bulk import from the Swedish heritage register, one item per ring. Britain and Ireland have no such import, so their circles are in the data only where volunteers have mapped them. My merged map holds 683 circles for Britain, Ireland and Brittany against the roughly 1,300 that archaeologists list, so about 53%. The Scandinavian lead is real in kind, since judge's rings are genuinely common, but the size of the lead is partly an accident of who digitised what.

04 · TraditionsSame ring, different habits

The traditions do not just differ in date. They sit in different places and different company. The table compares them on things the data can measure: how close they are to the sea, and how often a circle has another within 100 metres (a sign of cemeteries of many small rings rather than single big monuments).

Distance to the sea from the Natural Earth 10 m coastline. "Neighbour within 100 m": share of records with another record of the same tradition within 100 m. OSM period tags: how many records carry a mapper's period tag, and how many of those say Neolithic or Stone Age (N), Bronze Age (B) or Iron Age (I); a record can say more than one.

The Pyrenean rings are the clearest case of a different habit. 84% have another ring within a kilometre, and the typical gap to the next one is 200 m: they come in groups along the high pasture ridges, and almost none (1%) is within 5 km of the sea. Ship settings are the opposite: 63% are within 5 km of the coast, as you might expect of monuments shaped like boats. The circles of Britain and Ireland are more often single monuments: only 13% have another circle within 100 m. In Pomerania, 75% do, because the Gothic circles sit inside cemeteries.

Where OpenStreetMap mappers have tagged a period, they mostly agree with the published dates: 46 of 47 tagged Pyrenean rings say Iron Age, and all 21 tagged ship settings say Bronze Age.

06 · The questionAn island invention, or a shape people keep reinventing?

If the circle spread from one place, you would expect neighbouring traditions to touch in both space and time. So for each pair of traditions I measured the closest distance between any two of their records, and the gap (or overlap) between their published date ranges.

How far apart, and how many years apart?
Each point is a pair of traditions. Across: closest distance between any record of one and any record of the other. Up: years between their published date ranges; below the line, the ranges overlap.

Three pairs touch. Britain and Brittany are 116 km apart across the Channel and share the same dates. The Swedish judge's rings and the Pomeranian circles are 186 km apart across the Baltic and overlap by 325 years; Wikipedia's article on Iron Age stone circles notes that the Pomeranian practice "suggests Norse influence". Ship settings and judge's rings sit in the same fields.

Other pairs do not touch. The nearest British circle to any Scandinavian one is Hjaltadans in Shetland, 411 km of open sea from Domsteinane på Sola in Norway, and the published ranges leave a gap of about 400 years between the end of one and the start of the other. The Pyrenean rings begin while Britain's tradition is ending, but the closest records are 796 km apart. And the Alentejo cromlechs end, on their sources' dating, some 1,800 years before the Pyrenean rings begin, though they are only 178 km apart.

So my modest answer is: probably not one invention. The map is consistent with at least two separate beginnings, an Atlantic one in the Neolithic and a northern one in the Iron Age, with the Pyrenees as a possible third. Data like these cannot prove that people reinvented the ring; ideas can travel without leaving circles along the way, and many circles are missing from the data. But the simplest shape in monumental architecture does seem to have been found more than once.

07 · The groundDo British and Irish circles seek a view?

For Britain and Ireland I have a terrain model, so I could test one old idea: that circles were placed for their views. For each of the 669 circles I compared the spot with ten random land points 1 to 5 km away.

Circles against nearby ground
Share of circles that beat the median of their own ten comparison points. 50% would mean no preference.

The circles do sit a little higher than their surroundings. 65% of circles stand higher, relative to the ground within a kilometre, than the typical comparison point, and 28% are in the top quarter of their local ground, against 14% of comparison points. Their views are slightly wider: 60% see more of the ground within 5 km than their comparison points do. But the effect is gentle. The typical circle sees 23% of the ground around it against 21% for random spots nearby. Circle builders liked a shoulder or a low rise, not the summit.

08 · LimitsWhat this map cannot tell you

Coverage is uneven. Sweden's records come almost all from Wikidata, apparently a bulk import of the national heritage register; Britain's and Ireland's come mostly from volunteer mapping and hold about half the circles archaeologists list. Poland's Gothic circles are mapped one by one at some sites and not at all at others: Odry has twelve circles, ten of them whole, but only four are mapped. Counts across countries are counts of records, not of monuments.

One date per tradition, not per circle. Almost no record carries its own date. I give each tradition the range its sources give, which hides the variety inside it. The "Elsewhere" group (90 records, from Galicia and the Causses to Sardinia, Germany and Bulgaria) gets no date at all.

Radiocarbon dates are not building dates. A date at a circle may come from a burial, a hearth or a post-hole, from before or long after the stones were raised. The p3k14c coordinates are deliberately blurred, so I linked dates only by name and within 1 km, and I list every link for checking.

Classification is by place. I put a circle in a tradition by where it is and whether it is a ship setting. A Bronze Age circle in Sweden would be counted with the Iron Age judge's rings. Modern circles (millennium circles, Welsh Gorsedd stones, healing circles) are removed where their names or tags say so, but some may remain.

How I did this

Records. OpenStreetMap objects tagged megalith_type=stone_circle, cromlech or stone_ship, or historic=stone_circle (1,096 objects), plus objects whose name matches a stone circle word in several languages (Steinkreis, kamienny krąg, domarring, cromlech and others). Of 2,415 name matches, I kept 117: those with a historic tag and without street, business, trail, signboard or modern words in the name. In Britain and Ireland "cromlech" usually names a dolmen, so I dropped it there. From Wikidata I took items that are instances of stone circle and its subclasses (recumbent, four-poster) or of stone ship, 2,585 in Europe.

Duplicates. 180 OSM objects carry the Wikidata identifier of a Wikidata item and were merged with it; 98 more were merged with the nearest Wikidata item of the same form within 100 m (with 50 m it would be 98 matches, with 250 m 139). 48 OSM objects repeated another with the same name within 50 m. I removed 22 modern circles and 12 records outside Europe (Natural Earth continents).

Traditions. Britain, Ireland and the Isle of Man; Brittany (France west of 1° W and north of 47° N); the Pyrenees (Spain, France and Andorra between 42° and 43.6° N and 2.8° W to 3.3° E); south-west Iberia (Portugal and Spain south of 41.5° N); Scandinavia and Finland; Pomerania (Poland north of 53.4° N); ship settings wherever they are; and Elsewhere.

Dates. Published ranges: Britain, Ireland and Brittany 3300 to 900 BC, core 3200 to 2000 BC (Wikipedia, Stone circles in the British Isles and Brittany, citing Aubrey Burl); Alentejo from 6000 BC (Wikipedia, Almendres Cromlech; I end the faint band at 3000 BC as the source says the site runs into the Chalcolithic); Pyrenees Late Bronze Age about 1200 BC and through the Iron Age, with radiocarbon dates mostly in the first half of the first millennium BC (Mujika-Alustiza and others 2018); ship settings 1000 BC to AD 1000, mostly 1000 to 500 BC (Wikipedia, Stone ship); judge's rings 500 BC to AD 400 (Wikipedia, Stone circle (Iron Age)); Pomerania from the later first century AD to the end of the Wielbark culture about AD 400, with Odry and Grzybnica mostly used to about AD 200 (Wikipedia, Wielbark culture; Medieval Heritage). Radiocarbon: p3k14c sites within 1 km of a catalogue circle whose names share a distinctive word, calibrated with IntCal20; 115 candidate links within 5 km are listed with the reason each was kept or not.

Ground. AWS Terrain Tiles at about 90 m. Local rank: the share of 120 random points within 1 km that are lower. View: the share of 360 ground points (every 10° and every 500 m out to 5 km) in line of sight from 1.7 m eyes, with earth curvature and refraction. Paired Wilcoxon tests against ten random land points 1 to 5 km away per circle (p below 0.001 for local rank and view; p = 0.01 for height).
